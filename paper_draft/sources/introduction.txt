 1. Introduction
Stroke remains a leading cause of mortality and long-term disability worldwide. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, approximately 11.9 million incident strokes, 93.8 million prevalent cases, and 7.3 million stroke-related deaths occurred globally in 2021, underscoring the substantial and persistent burden of stroke on patients, families, and healthcare systems [1]. Although advances in acute stroke management have improved survival, many stroke survivors continue to experience motor impairment, restricted mobility, dysphagia, nutritional deterioration, and loss of independence. These complications may not only impede neurological recovery but also accelerate adverse changes in skeletal muscle structure and function.
Sarcopenia is a progressive skeletal muscle disorder characterized by reduced muscle strength, decreased muscle quantity or quality, and impaired physical performance. In contrast to primary sarcopenia, which is predominantly associated with aging, stroke-related sarcopenia is a secondary condition resulting from the combined effects of neurological injury and systemic metabolic disturbances. Central nervous system injury may lead to denervation, abnormal motor-unit recruitment, and impaired intermuscular coordination, whereas limb disuse, reduced physical activity, systemic inflammation, enhanced catabolism, and inadequate nutritional intake may further accelerate the loss of muscle mass and strength [2]. Consequently, stroke-related sarcopenia may develop rapidly and asymmetrically, with neurogenic, disuse-related, nutritional, and metabolic alterations occurring concurrently. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis further suggested that sex-specific factors, impaired proteostasis, and mitochondrial dysfunction may contribute to its development [3].
The prevalence of sarcopenia may increase substantially during the early stages after stroke. A systematic review examining the trajectory of sarcopenia before and after stroke estimated that its prevalence increased from 15.8% before stroke to 29.5% within 10 days after onset and to 51.6% between 10 days and 1 month after onset [4]. Similarly, an observational study of patients undergoing stroke rehabilitation reported a high prevalence of stroke-related sarcopenia and identified several potentially relevant clinical factors [5]. These findings indicate that skeletal muscle deterioration may begin soon after stroke rather than emerging exclusively as a chronic complication. The subacute phase therefore represents an important period during which neurological recovery, activity-dependent neuroplasticity, mobility restoration, nutritional changes, and muscle remodeling occur simultaneously.
During the subacute phase, prolonged bed rest and reduced use of the paretic limbs may rapidly induce disuse atrophy, whereas dysphagia, impaired oral intake, and insufficient nutritional provision may further promote protein–energy deficiency and catabolism. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis identified malnutrition risk, greater neurological impairment, nasogastric tube feeding, reduced walking ability, and lower activities of daily living as potential factors associated with stroke-related sarcopenia [6]. Moreover, the development of hospital-associated sarcopenia may be influenced by the balance between energy intake and the amount of rehabilitation provided during hospitalization [7]. These findings highlight the interaction among neurological severity, nutritional status, rehabilitation exposure, and skeletal muscle deterioration and support the need for early multidimensional assessment.
Sarcopenia and dysphagia may also interact bidirectionally after stroke. Loss of systemic and swallowing-related muscle mass may impair swallowing recovery, whereas persistent dysphagia may restrict energy and protein intake and consequently aggravate muscle loss. A multicenter cohort study demonstrated that sarcopenia was independently associated with poorer recovery of swallowing function among post-stroke patients with severe dysphagia [8]. In addition, baseline malnutrition diagnosed using the Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition criteria was associated with poorer recovery of activities of daily living among older patients with post-acute stroke [9]. Early identification of sarcopenia and its associated characteristics during the subacute phase may therefore facilitate timely nutritional and rehabilitative interventions before substantial muscle and functional deterioration becomes established.
Current diagnostic frameworks for sarcopenia are primarily based on the combined assessment of muscle strength, muscle quantity or quality, and physical performance. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry and bioelectrical impedance analysis are commonly used to estimate whole-body or appendicular muscle mass, whereas handgrip strength, gait speed, and chair-stand tests are used to assess muscle strength and physical performance. However, these conventional measurements may be difficult to interpret in patients with stroke. Hemiparesis, spasticity, impaired motor control, poor balance, and inability to walk independently may affect the feasibility and validity of performance-based assessments. Moreover, global or appendicular muscle mass measurements provide limited information regarding regional muscle quality, interlimb asymmetry, neuromuscular activation, and early functional alterations. Recent evidence suggests that muscle weakness may be more strongly associated with functional outcomes after stroke than either reduced muscle mass or conventionally defined sarcopenia [10]. Accordingly, reliance on a single anatomical or functional indicator may be insufficient to characterize the heterogeneous manifestations of stroke-related sarcopenia.
A multimodal assessment framework may provide a more comprehensive representation of post-stroke skeletal muscle deterioration by integrating morphological, electrophysiological, biological, nutritional, and clinical information. Muscle ultrasonography is noninvasive, portable, relatively inexpensive, and suitable for repeated bedside assessment. Ultrasound-derived parameters, including muscle thickness, cross-sectional area, and echo intensity, can provide complementary information regarding regional muscle quantity and tissue quality. Preliminary evidence has demonstrated the feasibility of ultrasound for evaluating muscle alterations associated with stroke-related sarcopenia [11]. Furthermore, a recent meta-analysis found that ultrasound-derived muscle thickness, cross-sectional area, and echo intensity were moderately to strongly associated with muscle strength, although their relationships with physical performance varied across different functional measures [12]. These findings suggest that muscle ultrasound may complement conventional measurements but should be interpreted together with functional and clinical indicators.
Ultrasound assessment of swallowing-related muscles may be particularly relevant in patients with stroke because conventional appendicular muscle measurements do not directly reflect the structural condition of muscles involved in mastication and swallowing. Recent evidence indicates that the mass of both swallowing and non-swallowing muscles may contribute to post-stroke swallowing function [13]. Evaluating muscles such as the geniohyoid, suprahyoid muscle group, anterior belly of the digastric, temporalis, and masseter may therefore provide additional information regarding the relationship among systemic muscle deterioration, dysphagia, and nutritional impairment. However, the extent to which swallowing-related ultrasound parameters contribute independently to the identification and prognosis of stroke-related sarcopenia remains insufficiently understood.
Unlike muscle ultrasound, which predominantly captures morphological characteristics, surface electromyography (sEMG) records the electrophysiological activity generated during muscle activation. It can therefore provide information regarding motor-unit recruitment, activation intensity, intermuscular coordination, signal complexity, and muscle fatigue—neuromuscular characteristics that cannot be adequately represented by muscle mass alone. Time-domain, frequency-domain, and time–frequency features derived from sEMG, including root mean square, mean absolute value, integrated electromyography, waveform length, zero crossings, slope sign changes, wavelet power, kurtosis, and wavelet entropy, may enable quantitative characterization of subtle neuromuscular alterations. A recent systematic review concluded that sEMG has considerable potential for the detection and monitoring of sarcopenia-related neuromuscular abnormalities, while also emphasizing substantial heterogeneity in the muscles examined, motor tasks, signal-processing procedures, and analytical methods used across studies [14].
Machine-learning methods offer a potentially useful framework for integrating these high-dimensional and heterogeneous data. In contrast to conventional regression approaches that generally focus on prespecified independent associations and commonly assume relatively simple functional relationships, machine-learning algorithms can accommodate nonlinear patterns and complex interactions among predictors. A recent study used forearm sEMG features to develop machine-learning models for sarcopenia screening among community-dwelling older adults and demonstrated the feasibility of identifying sarcopenia-related neuromuscular patterns from upper-extremity signals [15]. Another study combined empirical mode decomposition, feature selection, and multiple classification algorithms to distinguish individuals at different levels of sarcopenia risk across several motor tasks [16]. Nevertheless, most existing models have been developed in community-based or non-stroke populations, often using relatively small samples and a single type of biological signal.
More broadly, the available literature on stroke-related sarcopenia remains heterogeneous with respect to study populations, diagnostic criteria, disease stages, candidate predictors, and clinical outcomes [17]. Few studies have integrated sEMG, muscle ultrasonography, inflammatory and biochemical biomarkers, nutritional indicators, swallowing-related measurements, and conventional clinical characteristics within a unified analytical framework. Furthermore, previous prediction studies have predominantly focused on cross-sectional screening or classification. Whether multimodal characteristics measured during the subacute phase can predict subsequent recovery of activities of daily living, global disability, swallowing function, nutritional status, falls, or hospital readmission remains insufficiently investigated. A combined cross-sectional and prospective design is therefore required to distinguish factors associated with concurrent sarcopenia from factors that contribute to longer-term prognosis.
Accordingly, the present study combines a cross-sectional investigation with a prospective cohort component to evaluate stroke-related sarcopenia from both risk-assessment and prognostic perspectives. First, we aim to investigate the associations of sEMG characteristics, muscle ultrasound parameters, inflammatory and biochemical biomarkers, nutritional indicators, swallowing function, and conventional clinical variables with sarcopenia in patients with subacute stroke. Machine-learning models will subsequently be developed and internally validated to identify patients with concurrent sarcopenia or a high-risk multimodal profile. Second, participants will be prospectively followed for 6 months to determine whether baseline multimodal characteristics are associated with subsequent activities of daily living, global disability, swallowing function, nutritional status, falls, and hospital readmission and to evaluate their value for longitudinal prognostic stratification. By integrating neuromuscular, morphological, biological, nutritional, and functional information, this study seeks to establish a clinically interpretable framework for the early identification and prognostic assessment of stroke-related sarcopenia, thereby supporting individualized rehabilitation and precision nutritional management.
